About this book

Read it at the reporting station, not on the shelf.

Echocardiography guidelines are long, and the numbers you need are scattered across several of them. This book gathers those numbers in one place and keeps the reasoning attached to each one. Every chapter follows the same order: the criteria, the logic behind them, and the mistakes that catch out experienced readers.

It is written for cardiology fellows preparing for a lab rotation or an exam, and for consultants who want a quick, honest check before signing a report.

Sources

ASE/EACVI chamber quantification (2015), valvular stenosis and regurgitation (2017), ASE diastolic function and HFpEF update (2025), ASE adult reporting standards (2025), 2025 ESC/EACTS valvular guidelines, ACC/AHA 2020.

How the book handles disagreement

European and American documents agree on most thresholds and differ on a few. Where they differ, both values are printed and labelled. Where a number comes from one document only, the source is named in the table note.

Please verify

Classes of recommendation change between guideline editions. Before quoting one in a report or an exam, check it against the full current text.

Intervention thresholds in particular are moving. The early-intervention trials in asymptomatic aortic stenosis have already pushed practice ahead of older editions, so treat the decision tables here as a map of the landscape, and the guideline itself as the final authority.

01

Measuring the left ventricle

Measure it the same way every time, and the numbers become comparable across readers and across years.

Technique

Contrast is not a luxury. If two or more contiguous segments are poorly seen, use it, and say so in the report.

Volumes and ejection fraction come from the biplane method of discs (Simpson). Use apical four- and two-chamber views, trace the endocardium at end-diastole and end-systole, and keep the long axis uncut: a foreshortened apex underestimates volume and can hide an aneurysm.

Linear dimensions still matter for valve disease thresholds, particularly LV end-systolic diameter in regurgitant lesions. Measure them in the parasternal long axis, perpendicular to the LV long axis, at the tips of the mitral leaflets for end-diastole.

Index volumes, mass and left atrial size to body surface area. An unindexed LA volume in a small woman is a different number from the same volume in a large man.

Geometry in one picture

Why it matters

Concentric hypertrophy in the elderly should prompt a question about amyloid, hypertension, and aortic stenosis. It is rarely only one of them.

Combine LV mass and relative wall thickness before you label hypertrophy. The same mass can reflect pressure overload or volume overload, and the distinction changes the differential diagnosis.

Where strain earns its place

Global longitudinal strain detects dysfunction before the ejection fraction falls. Three settings justify the extra minute: cardio-oncology, where a relative fall of more than 15% from baseline is clinically meaningful; suspected cardiac amyloidosis, where apical sparing is a recognisable pattern; and aortic stenosis with a preserved ejection fraction.

Where it goes wrong

Comparing strain values across vendors, or from poorly tracked segments. Use one platform for serial studies and report which.

Report the vendor and software version beside the value, keep the frame rate high enough to track the whole cycle, and reject segments that the software has clearly mistracked.

02

Left atrial pressure and diastology

Estimate the pressure first. The grade is a label; the pressure is what changes management.

Algorithm

Use the flow chart in the ASE 2025 document for the stepwise logic. The table opposite lists its building blocks.

The 2025 ASE update restructures the approach. It asks whether left atrial pressure is normal or raised, then grades diastolic dysfunction, and it adds explicit guidance for diagnosing heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). The same parameters appear as before, but their context determines how much weight each deserves.

Before you apply any threshold, look at the patient: rhythm, mitral valve disease, annular calcification, prosthetic valves, bundle branch block and pacing all change how these measurements behave.

Grading, once the pressure is known

  • Grade I: impaired relaxation with normal left atrial pressure.
  • Grade II: pseudonormal filling with raised pressure.
  • Grade III: restrictive filling with markedly raised pressure.
Atrial fibrillation

Septal E/e′ of 11 or more, deceleration time of 160 ms or less, IVRT of 65 ms or less, and TR above 2.8 m/s all point to raised pressure.

A Valsalva manoeuvre helps unmask pseudonormal filling: a fall in E/A of 0.5 or more suggests that the baseline pattern was pseudonormal rather than normal.

Where it goes wrong

Applying E/e′ in moderate to severe mitral annular calcification, mitral stenosis, a mitral prosthesis, or severe primary MR. Rely on other markers there.

A useful question for a fellow at the station: is the left atrial pressure raised, and how do you know? An answer built on three agreeing parameters is better than a grade pasted from one.

03

The right heart

The right ventricle is an unforgiving window into the patient, and it is under-reported.

No single measurement describes right ventricular function well. Combine at least two: a longitudinal measure such as TAPSE or S′, and a global one such as fractional area change or free-wall strain. Disagreement between them is itself informative.

Estimating pulmonary pressure

Right atrial pressure

3 mmHg: IVC 21 mm or less and collapses more than 50%. 15 mmHg: IVC above 21 mm and collapses less than 50%. 8 mmHg: anything between.

Systolic pulmonary artery pressure is estimated as 4 × (TR peak velocity)² plus estimated right atrial pressure. The velocity carries most of the weight, so a clean continuous-wave envelope matters more than any other step.

The 2022 ESC/ERS guideline moves away from a single pressure cut-off and instead expresses the echocardiographic probability of pulmonary hypertension, using the TR velocity and a second group of signs.

Echocardiographic probability of PH, ESC/ERS 2022

2.9 to 3.4 m/sNoneIntermediate
2.9 to 3.4 m/sPresentHigh
Above 3.4 m/sNot requiredHigh

Other PH signs fall into three groups: ventricles (RV to LV basal ratio above 1.0, septal flattening with an eccentricity index above 1.1), pulmonary artery (RVOT acceleration time below 105 ms or mid-systolic notching, early diastolic pulmonary regurgitant velocity above 2.2 m/s, PA diameter above 25 mm), and venae cavae with right atrium (IVC above 21 mm with reduced collapse, right atrial area above 18 cm²).

Coupling

A TAPSE to PASP ratio below 0.55 mm/mmHg indicates RV to pulmonary arterial uncoupling and a worse prognosis.

Where it goes wrong

Reporting a pressure from a faint, incomplete TR envelope. A weak signal gives a falsely low velocity. Enhance it with agitated saline and state that you did.

04

Aortic stenosis

Grade it, define it, and never miss the low-flow patient.

Four numbers

Gradient, area, flow, ejection fraction. If these four do not tell one coherent story, one of the measurements is wrong.

Aortic stenosis is the valve lesion where echocardiography most directly decides treatment, and also the one where small technical errors change the category. This chapter follows the order in which you work: acquire, grade, resolve discordance, then decide.

4.1 Acquire three measurements, then add flow

Rule

Poor alignment always underestimates velocity. It never overestimates it.

  • Peak velocity. Continuous-wave Doppler from several windows (apical, right parasternal, suprasternal) with a dedicated non-imaging probe. Report the highest clean signal and name the window.
  • Mean gradient. Trace the same continuous-wave envelope. Do not mix gradients from different beats.
  • Valve area by continuity. The LVOT diameter is squared in the calculation, so a 1 mm error shifts the area by roughly 0.2 cm². Measure just below the valve in mid-systole.
  • Dimensionless velocity index. The ratio of LVOT to aortic VTI. It removes the LVOT diameter, and a value of 0.25 or below supports severe stenosis.
  • Stroke volume index. LVOT area times LVOT VTI divided by body surface area. A value below 35 mL/m² defines low flow.

4.2 Grade severity

Severe aortic stenosis is defined by a peak velocity of 4.0 m/s or more or a mean gradient of 40 mmHg or more, with a valve area of 1.0 cm² or less (indexed area 0.6 cm²/m² or less). The ladder below places the other categories on the same axis.

Critical aortic stenosis

In adults, critical (or very severe) stenosis means a peak velocity of 5.0 m/s or more and / or a mean gradient of 60 mmHg or more, usually with a valve area below 0.6 cm². ACC/AHA 2020 uses the term very severe. ESC/EACTS treats a velocity above 5.0 m/s as a high-risk marker that supports early intervention even without symptoms.

Be precise in the report. No society defines the word critical by one universal number. Give the measured values rather than the label alone.

In neonates, critical aortic stenosis is a different entity: a duct-dependent systemic circulation presenting in the first days of life. Adult cut-offs do not apply.

Mind the flow. A very high gradient needs adequate flow. A patient can have a critically narrow valve and a modest gradient when stroke volume is low.

4.3 When the numbers disagree

About one third of patients with a valve area of 1.0 cm² or less have a mean gradient below 40 mmHg. Do not average the two categories. Ask about flow first, then about the ejection fraction.

4.4 The two types of low-flow, low-gradient stenosis

Both types share the same three numbers (valve area 1.0 cm² or less, mean gradient below 40 mmHg, stroke volume index below 35 mL/m²). They separate on the ejection fraction, and the diagnostic pathway differs.

Dobutamine stress echo in the classical type

Start at 2.5 to 5 µg/kg/min and increase every 3 to 5 minutes to a maximum of 20 µg/kg/min, recording stroke volume, Vmax, mean gradient and valve area at each stage.

Projected AVA

When flow rise is modest, compute the valve area projected at a normal flow rate of 250 mL/s: AVArest + (AVApeak − AVArest) / (Qpeak − Qrest) × (250 − Qrest). Severe if below 1.0 cm².

  • Flow (contractile) reserve is present when stroke volume rises by 20% or more.
  • True-severe AS: the valve area stays at 1.0 cm² or less, with a peak velocity of 4 m/s or more (or a mean gradient of 40 mmHg or more) at the higher flow.
  • Pseudo-severe AS: the valve area rises above about 1.0 to 1.2 cm² as flow improves, and the gradient remains low.
  • No flow reserve: the test cannot decide. Operative risk is higher, but benefit from valve replacement persists. Use the CT calcium score to adjudicate.

Checklist before accepting paradoxical low-flow

  • Blood pressure. Scan with controlled systolic pressure (below 140 mmHg). Hypertension lowers flow and distorts gradients.
  • LVOT area. The outflow tract is elliptical. A single 2D diameter underestimates its area, and that lowers both the valve area and the stroke volume. Cross-check with 3D echo or CT.
  • Body size. Index the valve area in small patients. An area of 0.9 cm² in a very small woman may be moderate stenosis.
  • Rhythm. Average at least five beats in atrial fibrillation.
  • Mimics and companions. Amyloidosis, significant mitral regurgitation, mitral stenosis, or aortic regurgitation can reproduce the picture. Pressure recovery can lower gradients in a small aorta (below 30 mm).
Third group

Normal-flow, low-gradient AS (SVi 35 or more, LVEF 50% or more) is usually moderate stenosis or a measurement inconsistency. Re-measure before you grade it severe.

That third group is where the valve-area cut-off and the gradient cut-off simply do not correspond. Check the LVOT diameter, repeat the continuous-wave recording from another window, and compare with the dimensionless index.

4.5 Calcium score

Non-contrast CT measurement of aortic valve calcium (Agatston units) is independent of flow, which makes it valuable in exactly the situations where echo is discordant. ESC/EACTS recommends sex-specific thresholds.

Calcium confirms a burden; it does not replace haemodynamics. Severe stenosis can occur with less calcium, particularly in younger patients, bicuspid valves, and rheumatic disease.

Surveillance

Mild: every 3 to 5 years. Moderate: every 1 to 2 years. Severe, asymptomatic: every 6 to 12 months, with symptom education. Shorten the interval when velocity is high, calcification is heavy, or progression is fast.

Surveillance intervals above are typical practice rather than strict rules. Heavy calcification and a velocity above 4.5 m/s move a patient towards a shorter interval.

4.7 The five-line AS report

  1. Valve morphology (tricuspid, bicuspid, rheumatic) and degree of calcification.
  2. Vmax, mean gradient, AVA, AVAi and DVI, with the windows used.
  3. LVOT diameter and stroke volume index (the flow state).
  4. LVEF and strain, LV mass, relative wall thickness.
  5. One sentence on severity, flow state, and what would change management.

Aortic regurgitation

No single parameter is enough. Integrate.

Grade aortic regurgitation from the mechanism, a qualitative impression, and at least two semi-quantitative or quantitative measures. Colour jet size alone is the least reliable of these.

Timing of surgery in chronic severe AR

Do not forget

Measure the aortic root and ascending aorta. A dilated root changes the surgical threshold independently of the valve.

Symptoms are an indication. In asymptomatic patients, decisions rest on LV size and function. The two societies differ slightly:

  • ESC/EACTS 2021: LV end-systolic diameter above 50 mm (or above 25 mm/m² in small patients), or resting LVEF of 50% or less.
  • ACC/AHA 2020: LVEF of 55% or less (class I); LV end-systolic diameter above 50 mm (class IIa).
  • The 2025 ESC/EACTS text should be checked for updated cut-offs.
Where it goes wrong

Underestimating eccentric jets with colour area. Use the vena contracta, flow reversal in the descending aorta, and PISA instead.

In acute severe regurgitation the LV is not dilated. A very short pressure half-time and early mitral valve closure are clues, and these patients need urgent surgery rather than serial imaging.

Mitral stenosis

Rheumatic mitral stenosis has not gone away. Look for it, especially in patients from endemic regions.

Severity of mitral stenosis
ParameterMildModerateClinically significant (severe)
Valve area, planimetryAbove 1.5 cm²1.0 to 1.5 cm²1.5 cm² or less; very severe 1.0 cm² or less
Pressure half-timeBelow 150 ms150 to 219 ms150 ms or more; very severe 220 ms or more
Mean gradientBelow 5 mmHg5 to 10 mmHgAbove 10 mmHg at a heart rate of 60 to 80
Systolic PA pressureBelow 30 mmHg30 to 50 mmHgAbove 50 mmHg

Technique

Heart rate

Always record the heart rate with the gradient. The same orifice can give very different mean gradients at 60 and 110 beats per minute.

  • Planimetry in the parasternal short axis, at the leaflet tips, with the lowest gain that shows the orifice. It is the reference method when the window allows.
  • Pressure half-time gives area as 220 divided by the half-time. It is unreliable in atrial fibrillation, tachycardia, abnormal chamber compliance, after balloon valvotomy, and with significant aortic regurgitation.
  • Continuity or PISA help when planimetry fails.
  • Wilkins score: mobility, thickening, calcification and subvalvular thickening, each scored 1 to 4. A total of 8 or less favours percutaneous balloon commissurotomy.

Before balloon commissurotomy

Exclude left atrial or appendage thrombus with transoesophageal echo and confirm that mitral regurgitation is no more than mild. Intervention is indicated in symptomatic patients with clinically significant stenosis and favourable anatomy, and may be considered in asymptomatic patients with a high thromboembolic risk or pulmonary hypertension (systolic pressure above 50 mmHg).

Degenerative MS

Annular calcification that extends into the leaflets causes stenosis in older patients. Pressure half-time is unreliable here, so use planimetry or continuity, and consider CT.

Mitral regurgitation

First ask whether it is primary or secondary. The answer changes the thresholds and the treatment.

Primary MR: when to operate

Where it goes wrong

Wall-hugging jets (the Coanda effect) are underestimated by colour area. Use vena contracta, pulmonary vein flow and quantitative volumes, and move to TEE or CMR when in doubt.

  • Symptomatic severe MR with LVEF above 30%: surgery, with repair preferred when durable repair is feasible.
  • Asymptomatic with LV end-systolic diameter of 40 mm or more, or LVEF of 60% or less: surgery.
  • Asymptomatic with new atrial fibrillation, resting systolic PA pressure above 50 mmHg, or LA volume index of 60 mL/m² or more: surgery should be considered in a centre with a high repair rate.

Secondary (functional) MR

In secondary MR the leaflets are normal and the ventricle or the annulus is diseased. The regurgitant orifice is elliptical, so lower thresholds are used: an EROA of 0.20 cm² or more, or a regurgitant volume of 30 mL or more, qualifies as severe in ESC practice. The COAPT trial enrolled patients with an EROA above 0.30 cm².

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair is considered in symptomatic patients on optimal guideline-directed therapy whose anatomy suits the device. Follow the COAPT selection profile: LVEF 20 to 50%, LV end-systolic diameter 70 mm or less, systolic PA pressure 70 mmHg or less.

Tricuspid regurgitation

Severe is no longer the top of the scale.

Many patients now reach tricuspid intervention after years of untreated severe regurgitation, and outcomes differ strikingly within the old severe category. The five-grade scheme separates them.

Five-grade scheme for TR (ASE 2017; Hahn and Zamorano 2017)
ParameterMildModerateSevereMassiveTorrential
Vena contractaBelow 3 mm3 to 6.9 mm7 to 13 mm14 to 20 mm21 mm or more
EROABelow 20 mm²20 to 39 mm²40 to 59 mm²60 to 79 mm²80 mm² or more
Regurgitant volumeBelow 30 mL30 to 44 mL45 to 59 mL60 to 74 mL75 mL or more

PISA radius at a Nyquist limit of about 28 cm/s.

Supportive signs

Dense triangular CW signal with early peaking, hepatic vein systolic reversal, E velocity above 1.0 m/s, annulus above 40 mm, dilated RA, RV and IVC.

Always state the mechanism: secondary (annular dilation, the commonest), primary, or related to a device lead. Report the right ventricular size and function and the rhythm alongside the grade.

Where it goes wrong

Reading a low TR velocity as a low pulmonary pressure. In free-flowing TR the velocity is low because the pressures have equalised.

09

The report

A report is a message to the person who will decide what happens next.

The structure below is consistent with the ASE 2025 standards for adult echocardiography reporting. Adapt it to your laboratory template, but keep the order: the question first, the evidence next, the conclusion last and short.

  1. Indication and clinical question, image quality, rhythm, heart rate, blood pressure and body surface area.
  2. Left ventricle: size, mass and geometry, method for ejection fraction, regional wall motion, strain if measured.
  3. Diastology: is left atrial pressure raised, and which parameters support it.
  4. Right ventricle: size, function, estimated systolic PA pressure, probability of pulmonary hypertension.
  5. Atria, IVC, pericardium, aortic root and ascending aorta.
  6. Each valve: mechanism, severity, the parameters used, and the consequence for the chambers.
  7. Conclusion in three to five lines, with the first line answering the clinical question, and comparison with the previous study.

Before you sign

  1. Did the report answer the referral question?
  2. Are volumes and ejection fraction indexed and compared with the last study?
  3. If aortic stenosis: is the stroke volume index reported and the flow state named?
  4. If MR or TR: are the mechanism and the quantitative parameters listed?
  5. Are right atrial pressure, PA pressure and the PH probability stated?
  6. Do the cut-offs quoted match the current guideline editions?
